## Overview

This project is a **commercial credit risk pipeline** that gives a loan officer two independent views of each borrower: a machine learning model that scores default risk from the application financials, and an LLM advisory that reads external news and flags adverse signals the financials cannot capture.

I built it twice: first by hand in Python, covering data preparation, model comparison, scoring, the LLM calls, serving, and drift monitoring, and then again on Dataiku DSS, a managed ML platform, using the same data and design. Building it both ways was a way to understand where a platform like this saves time and effort, and which parts of the work remain with the engineer.

The core design principle is **two independent signals, one human decision**. The model and the advisory never feed into each other, and only meet in the officer's review table.

<details>
<summary><strong>Problem Framing</strong></summary>

A credit model can only assess what is on the application. A borrower can look healthy on paper, with decades of trading history, strong debt coverage, and no missed payments, while facing risks the application never asks about: a major customer entering insolvency, a sector in its third straight quarterly decline, or a regulatory probe into leadership.

Instead of asking *“will this borrower default, based on their financials?”*, the project frames the problem as:

> *How can a credit officer see both what the model can score and what it can't, without letting an unpredictable signal into a regulated decision?*

Language models are well suited to reading unstructured news, but their output is not deterministic. For that reason the advisory is kept strictly separate from the model: its findings are shown to the officer and are never used as a model input.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Data & Features</strong></summary>

The pipeline takes two inputs:

- **Loan applications** - 2,000 commercial borrowers across ten sectors, with financials (revenue, loan size, margin), ratios (debt coverage, leverage, liquidity), repayment history, and a default / no-default label. This feeds the model.
- **External context** - a short plain-language news summary for 40 borrowers, standing in for the news and watchlist screening feed a bank would use in production. This feeds the advisory.

Each external context record also carries a ground-truth label indicating whether it should be flagged, so the advisory's output can be checked against a known answer.

All data is synthetic, designed to carry realistic signal without using real company names. A third *downturn* dataset, containing the same borrowers with weaker financials, is used to test drift monitoring.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Modelling Approach</strong></summary>

Five classifiers were trained and compared on the same held-out set: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Decision Tree, and XGBoost.

Logistic Regression performed best, with a ROC AUC of 0.86. Credit risk tends to rise and fall steadily with its drivers, which suits a linear model, and logistic regression is also the easiest model to explain to a risk committee or regulator.

Two further decisions shaped how the model is used:

- **Evaluation metric.** With a default rate of around 15%, a model that approves everyone is 85% accurate and catches no defaults, so the models were compared on AUC, with precision and recall reported at the chosen threshold.
- **Decision threshold.** Borrowers are flagged at a 30% predicted default probability, roughly twice the average risk. Setting this line is a trade-off between the cost of a missed default and the cost of an extra manual review, and in practice would be agreed with the credit team.

The strongest drivers (leverage, prior delinquencies, and debt service coverage) are consistent with how a credit analyst would typically rank risk.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>The Advisory</strong></summary>

For each borrower, an LLM reads the external context and returns a structured assessment:

- whether there is an adverse signal
- its type (customer, sector, leadership, litigation, or credit deterioration)
- its severity
- a one-line note for the officer
- whether closer review is recommended

The advisory never sees the model's score or the ground-truth label, and it makes no lending decisions. Its output is parsed into typed fields before being shown, so the officer receives a consistent record for every borrower.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Serving & Monitoring</strong></summary>

The hand-built version also includes two production components: a **FastAPI** endpoint that scores a single live application, and a **drift check** that compares incoming applications to the training data and flags significant shifts.

The main challenge in serving was consistency. The preprocessing fitted during training has to be saved and applied identically to every live request; if the two fall out of step, the model still returns a score, but the score is wrong. For monitoring, the downturn dataset was run through the drift check, which flagged the shifted financial ratios while leaving stable features such as sector unflagged.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Rebuilding on a Managed Platform</strong></summary>

With the hand-built version complete, I rebuilt the same pipeline on Dataiku DSS, using the same data, model choice, and advisory design. The hand-built version took roughly **ten hours**; the platform version took about **three**.

Most of the time saved came from infrastructure work that the platform provides out of the box. Seven components I had written by hand were handled natively:

- **Preprocessing at scoring time** - packaged with the trained model
- **Comparing five models** - AutoML
- **Prompting the LLM and parsing its output** - an LLM recipe and visual data preparation
- **Connecting the stages** - the visual Flow
- **Containerising the runtime** - managed execution and deployment
- **Separating the model and advisory** - visible in the structure of the Flow
- **Drift monitoring** - evaluation stores and drift analysis

Three differences stood out in practice:

- **Mixed interfaces on one pipeline.** I used Python where I wanted fine control and visual steps elsewhere, in a single project that an analyst and an engineer could both work in.
- **Changing LLM provider.** In the hand-built version the provider is hardcoded, and moving from OpenAI to Anthropic would mean rewriting the client, authentication, and response handling. On the platform it was a configuration change.
- **Visible governance.** The separation between model and advisory, which I had enforced in code, could be seen directly in the Flow, with data lineage tracked automatically.

The design decisions, from framing the problem to choosing the threshold and bounding the advisory, were the same in both builds. The platform's contribution was in the surrounding infrastructure, which is also where most of the long-term maintenance effort sits.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Example Output</strong></summary>

Ember Outlets is a representative case. On paper it looks sound, with $79.4M in revenue, debt service coverage of 1.49x, and no missed payments, and the model scores it well below the review threshold.

The advisory flags it:

```json
{
  "borrower_id": "B1122",
  "adverse_signal": true,
  "signal_type": "customer_risk",
  "severity": "high",
  "advisory_note": "Ember Outlets faces a material customer concentration risk because its largest customer has entered insolvency proceedings, which could significantly reduce revenue and cash flow.",
  "recommend_review": true
}
```

The model score stays as it is. The officer sees the score and the advisory together, and decides whether the customer concentration risk warrants a closer look before approval.

</details>
